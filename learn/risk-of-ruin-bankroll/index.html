Bankroll Math and Risk of Ruin: How Long Will Your Money Last?

How long a gambling budget lasts depends on edge, bet size and variance. Use the classic ruin formula and worked roulette examples to see your real odds.

By e.casino Editorial TeamUpdated Nov 2, 20268 min read

Key takeaways

  • Two numbers drive every bankroll: the drift (expected loss per bet) and the swing (standard deviation per bet).
  • With $10 bets on European red for four hours, a $100 bankroll goes broke about 51% of the time, a $300 bankroll about 2.4% of the time.
  • Trying to turn $100 into $200 on European red succeeds 48.6% of the time with one bet but only 0.45% of the time with $1 bets.
  • Even with a genuine 1% edge, a 50-unit bankroll has about a 37% risk of ruin; advantage play needs deep pockets.

How long your money lasts depends on three things: the house edge, the size of your bets, and how wildly the game swings. The edge decides where you are heading; the swings decide how bumpy the road is and how likely you are to hit zero along the way. Risk of ruin is the math that puts a number on that.

The two numbers that run your bankroll

Every bet has a drift, its expected loss, and a swing, its standard deviation. For a $10 bet on red in European roulette:

  • Drift: −$10 × 1/37 ≈ −$0.27 per bet.
  • Swing: about $10 per bet, since each spin ends close to $10 up or $10 down.

Over many bets, drift adds up in a straight line while swing grows with the square root of the number of bets.

After N bets: expected result = N × drift, standard deviation = swing × √N

At a commonly cited 35 spins an hour, one hour of $10 red bets has an expected result of −$9.46 and a standard deviation of $59.14. Over four hours (140 spins), it is −$37.84 with a standard deviation of $118.28.

That is the key fact about short sessions: the swing is several times larger than the drift. Over four hours, the luck component dwarfs the edge. Over hundreds of hours, it reverses, as explained in the law of large numbers guide.

Will your bankroll survive four hours?

Now add a hard floor: if your money hits zero, the night is over. Here is what happens to three bankrolls betting $10 on European red for up to 140 spins, worked out exactly by tracking every possible path spin by spin.

Look at the last column. A bigger bankroll barely changes your chance of finishing ahead. What it changes is your chance of being knocked out early. With $100 against $10 bets, you have only a ten-loss cushion, and a coin-flip chance of busting before the four hours are up.

The gambler's ruin formula

A classic problem asks: if you start with some money and stop when you either double it or lose it all, what is your chance of success? For even-money bets, with win probability p, loss probability q = 1 − p, a start of i betting units and a target of N units:

P(reach N before 0) = (1 − r^i) ÷ (1 − r^N), where r = q ÷ p

On a European wheel, r = (19/37) ÷ (18/37) = 19/18 ≈ 1.0556. In a fair game r = 1 and the formula simplifies to i ÷ N, so a fair 50/50 bet doubles you exactly half the time, whatever your bet size.

The math: With $10 bets, i = 10 and N = 20, so P = (1 − 1.0556^10) ÷ (1 − 1.0556^20) = 1 ÷ (1 + 1.0556^10) ≈ 1 ÷ 2.717 ≈ 36.8%. The expected loss column is the house edge times the expected total wagered: 98 spins × $10 ÷ 37 ≈ $26.39, which matches $100 − (36.8% × $200).

The pattern surprises most people. In a negative-edge game, small bets give the edge more spins to work, so they make reaching a target less likely, not more. With $1 bets, you would play for over 100 hours on average and almost certainly lose the $100.

That is not a recommendation to bet big. Every row has a negative expected result. Small bets buy more playing time; they do not buy better odds.

What a win target really does

The same formula shows what happens when you set a stop-win target. Start with $200, bet $10 on European red, and quit when you either reach the target or go broke:

  • Target +$50: you get there about 68.0% of the time, after about 111 spins on average. Expected loss: $29.90.
  • Target +$100: about 48.0%, after about 208 spins. Expected loss: $56.13.
  • Target +$200: about 25.3%, after about 365 spins. Expected loss: $98.70.

A modest target makes a winning night common, which feels like a strategy. It is not. Two times in three you win $50, and one time in three you lose $200. The expected loss is still exactly 2.70% of everything wagered, and a bigger target simply means more spins and a bigger expected loss.

Risk of ruin, even with an edge

Advantage players, such as skilled card counters, face ruin too. Take a hypothetical even-money game with a genuine 1% edge for the player: you win 50.5% of the time. If you never stop, the chance of eventually losing a bankroll of B units is:

Risk of ruin = (q ÷ p)^B ≈ e^(−2 × edge × B ÷ variance per bet)

With q ÷ p = 0.495 ÷ 0.505 ≈ 0.9802:

  • 25 units: about 60.7% risk of ruin
  • 50 units: about 36.8%
  • 100 units: about 13.5%
  • 200 units: about 1.8%
  • 400 units: about 0.03%

Even with the math on your side, a thin bankroll gets wiped out more often than not. That is why real advantage play needs a bankroll of hundreds of betting units, and why it is much harder than it looks. With a negative edge and no stopping point, the risk of ruin is simply 100%.

Turning the math into a budget

If you choose to gamble for entertainment, bankroll math gives you a practical way to set limits:

  1. Estimate the expected cost: bet × bets per hour × hours × house edge. Four hours of $10 European red: $10 × 140 × 2.70% ≈ $38.
  2. Add a buffer for swings: about two standard deviations. For 140 spins, 2 × $118 ≈ $237, so a budget near $300 covers a bad-but-normal night. With $300, the table above shows only a 2.4% chance of going broke in four hours.
  3. Decide in advance that the money is spent. A loss limit does not change the house edge, but it caps the damage and makes the number real before you start.
Tip: Run your own numbers in the free bankroll calculator, which shows expected loss, variance, the chance of being ahead and risk of ruin for your game and bet size.
Reality check: No bankroll size makes a negative-edge game profitable. A bigger bankroll only means it takes longer for the edge to win. The budget guide covers how to set limits you will actually keep.

The bottom line

Your bankroll is pushed down by the drift and shaken by the swing. Over short sessions the swing dominates, which is why a bigger bankroll mostly protects you from early knockouts rather than improving your odds of winning.

The gambler's ruin formula shows the uncomfortable truth: in a casino game, more bets means less chance of reaching a target, and with no stopping point, ruin is certain. Use the math to size a budget you can afford, and if keeping to it gets hard, help is available.

Frequently asked questions

What is risk of ruin?

Risk of ruin is the probability of losing your entire bankroll before reaching a goal or stopping point. It depends on the house edge, the size of each bet relative to the bankroll, and the variance of the game.

Do smaller bets make my money last longer?

Yes, smaller bets usually let you play longer before going broke. But in a negative-edge game, more bets also means more total wagered, so smaller bets lower your chance of reaching a fixed profit target.

What is the gambler's ruin formula?

For even-money bets with win probability p and loss probability q, starting with i units and aiming for N units, the chance of reaching N first is (1 − (q/p)^i) ÷ (1 − (q/p)^N). In a fair game this simplifies to i ÷ N.

How big should a gambling budget be?

Start with the expected loss for your planned play, then add a buffer for normal swings, roughly two standard deviations. Most importantly, it should be money you have already decided you can afford to lose.
