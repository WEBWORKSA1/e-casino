Texas Hold'em Odds: Outs, Pot Odds & the Rule of 2 and 4

Learn Texas Holdem odds: starting-hand math, counting outs, the rule of 2 and 4, pot odds and when a call is profitable, with exact percentages and examples.

By e.casino Editorial TeamUpdated Nov 2, 20266 min read

Key takeaways

  • Outs are the unseen cards that improve your hand to a likely winner. A flush draw has 9; an open-ended straight draw has 8.
  • Rule of 2 and 4: multiply outs by 2 for one card to come, or by 4 for two cards (flop to river). It is close for small draws and overstates big ones.
  • Pot odds compare the price of a call with the size of the pot. Call when your equity beats call divided by the final pot.
  • A 9-out flush draw hits by the river about 35% of the time; on a single card it is about 19.6%.

Hold'em decisions come down to two numbers: how likely you are to improve, and how much the pot is paying you to try. Count your outs, convert them to a percentage with the rule of 2 and 4, and compare that with your pot odds. If your chance of winning beats the price, the call makes money in the long run.

A quick refresher: how a hand unfolds

  1. Preflop. Each player gets two private hole cards, and there's a round of betting.
  2. The flop. Three shared community cards are dealt face up, then another betting round.
  3. The turn. A fourth community card, more betting.
  4. The river. A fifth community card and a final betting round.
  5. Showdown. Remaining players make their best five-card hand from any of their seven cards, using the usual hand rankings.

Unlike blackjack or roulette, you aren't betting against the house. You play other people, and the cardroom takes a fee, called the rake, from pots. Your edge comes only from making better decisions than your opponents.

Starting-hand odds

There are C(52, 2) = 1,326 possible two-card starting hands.

  • Any pocket pair: 13 ranks × 6 suit combinations = 78 hands. 78 ÷ 1,326 = 1/17, about 5.9%.
  • Pocket aces: 6 combinations, so 6 ÷ 1,326 = 1/221, about 0.45%.
  • Flopping a set with a pocket pair: the flop misses both of your remaining two cards with probability C(48, 3) ÷ C(50, 3) = 17,296 ÷ 19,600 ≈ 88.2%. So you flop at least a set about 11.8% of the time, roughly 1 flop in 8.5.

Even the best hand isn't safe. Pocket aces against pocket kings all-in before the flop wins about 82% of the time, so the kings still win nearly one time in five.

Counting outs

An out is an unseen card that turns your hand into a probable winner. Count them from the cards you can't see. After the flop that's 52 − 2 hole cards − 3 board cards = 47; opponents' hidden cards count as unseen because you don't know what they are.

  • Flush draw: four cards of a suit after the flop. 13 − 4 = 9 outs.
  • Open-ended straight draw: like 8-9-10-J, which any 7 or queen completes. 2 ranks × 4 = 8 outs.
  • Gutshot straight draw: like 8-9-J-Q, which needs exactly a 10. 4 outs.
  • Two overcards: A-K on a low board, hoping to pair. 3 aces + 3 kings = 6 outs.
  • Flush draw plus open-ender: 9 + 6 = 15 outs, because two of the eight straight cards are already counted in your suit.
Reality check: Some outs are "dirty." A card that completes your flush but pairs the board might give an opponent a full house. Discount outs that could help someone else, and don't count overcards as clean outs when the board looks dangerous.

The rule of 2 and 4

With 47 unseen cards after the flop (or 46 after the turn), exact math is slow at the table. The shortcut:

One card to come: outs × 2 ≈ % to hit. Two cards to come: outs × 4 ≈ % to hit.

Here's how the shortcut compares with the exact numbers. The one-card column uses 46 unseen cards (turn to river); the two-card column is flop to river, seeing both cards.

The math: For a 9-out flush draw from flop to river, you miss twice with probability (38/47) × (37/46) = 1,406/2,162 ≈ 65.0%. So you hit at least once about 35.0% of the time. The rule of 4 says 36%, close enough for a decision at the table.

The ×4 rule overshoots big draws because it double-counts the cases where both cards hit. A handy fix: above 8 outs, subtract (outs − 8). For 15 outs, 60 − 7 = 53%, near the exact 54.1%.

Pot odds: is the call worth it?

Pot odds tell you the minimum equity a call needs to break even:

Required equity = amount to call ÷ (pot after your call)

The most common slip is dividing by the wrong pot. Your call goes into the pot too, so the denominator includes it. Calling $20 when the pot holds $100 is a 20 ÷ 120 decision, not 20 ÷ 100.

Example 1: a good price

On the turn, the pot is $80. Your opponent bets $20, so the pot is $100 and you must call $20. After your call it holds $120, so you need 20 ÷ 120 ≈ 16.7%. Your flush draw hits the river 9/46 ≈ 19.6% of the time.

EV of calling = (9/46 × $100) − (37/46 × $20) = $160 ÷ 46 ≈ +$3.48. Call.

Example 2: a bad price

Same draw, but the opponent bets $60 into $60. You must call $60 for a final pot of $180, so you need 33.3%. You have 19.6%.

EV of calling = (9/46 × $120) − (37/46 × $60) ≈ −$24.78. Fold, unless you're confident of winning much more later.

Example 3: all-in on the flop

The pot is $100 and an opponent moves all-in for $50. You need 50 ÷ 200 = 25%. With a flush draw and both cards guaranteed, you have about 35%. EV ≈ 0.35 × $150 − 0.65 × $50 ≈ +$20. Call.

Tip: Pot odds are often quoted as a ratio, like "5 to 1" for calling $20 to win $100. Convert with 1 ÷ (5 + 1) = 16.7%. The odds converter does this instantly.

Implied odds and the limits of the math

Implied odds count the money you expect to win on later streets when you hit. They can justify a call that pot odds alone reject, as in Example 2, but only if your opponent will actually pay you off.

Reverse implied odds are the opposite: you hit and still lose, for example making a small flush against a bigger one. Those turn apparent profit into losses.

The outs-and-pot-odds method assumes you know what you're drawing against. In real hands you're estimating an opponent's range, so treat the numbers as a compass, not a GPS.

Practicing the numbers

Drill outs on random boards, and check your estimates against exact equities with the free poker odds calculator. Comparing your gut with the math is the fastest way to train the gut.

For the general idea behind every calculation here, see expected value for beginners: a call is good when its average outcome is positive, regardless of how this particular river falls.

The bottom line

Hold'em math is counting plus one division. Count clean outs, use the rule of 2 and 4 to estimate your chance, and compare it with the call divided by the final pot. Call when you're getting the right price, fold when you aren't.

The math won't stop bad beats; a 35% draw still misses 65% of the time. What it does is make sure that over many hands, your decisions are worth money rather than costing it.

Frequently asked questions

What is the rule of 2 and 4 in poker?

A shortcut for estimating your chance of hitting a draw. With one card to come, multiply your outs by 2; with two cards to come, multiply by 4. Nine outs gives about 18% and 36%, versus the exact 19.6% and 35%.

How do you calculate pot odds?

Divide the amount you must call by the total pot after your call. Calling $20 into a pot that will hold $120 means you need at least 20 divided by 120, or about 16.7% equity, to break even.

What are the odds of hitting a flush draw?

With 9 outs on the flop, about 35% by the river if you see both cards. With one card to come, about 19.1% on the turn or 19.6% on the river.

How often are you dealt pocket aces?

Once every 221 hands on average, or about 0.45%. Any pocket pair arrives once every 17 hands, about 5.9%.
