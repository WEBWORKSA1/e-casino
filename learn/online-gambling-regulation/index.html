How Gambling Regulation Works Around the World

Licensing, game testing, player protections and the state-by-state US system: how gambling regulation works, and what a license can and cannot promise you.

By e.casino Editorial TeamUpdated Nov 2, 20267 min read

Key takeaways

  • Governments regulate gambling to protect players, keep crime out, ensure games are fair and collect tax.
  • Common models include prohibition, state monopolies and competitive licensing, each with different trade-offs.
  • Licensed operators typically face background checks, independent game testing, player-fund rules and responsible-gambling requirements.
  • A license confirms the game works as advertised. It does not remove the house edge or make gambling a way to earn money.

Gambling regulation exists to answer three questions: is the game honest, is the money safe, and are players protected? Different countries answer them in different ways, from outright bans to state monopolies to open licensing markets. What no regulator does is remove the house edge. A licensed game is a fair game, and a fair casino game still favors the casino.

Why governments regulate gambling

Gambling combines large flows of cash, games that are hard for players to verify and a product some people struggle to control. That mix attracts government attention for several reasons.

  • Fairness: Players cannot inspect a shuffling machine or a random number generator. Someone has to.
  • Crime prevention: Cash-heavy businesses can be used to launder money, so regulators impose identity checks and reporting rules.
  • Player protection: Rules on age, advertising and responsible-gambling tools aim to reduce harm.
  • Revenue: Gambling is usually taxed, often at higher rates than ordinary businesses.

The idea is old. Venice's Il Ridotto, opened in 1638, was an early attempt to bring gambling into a supervised, sanctioned venue, as covered in our history of casinos.

The main regulatory models

Most countries use one of a handful of approaches, sometimes mixing several for different products such as lotteries, casinos and sports betting.

Common approaches to gambling regulation
ModelHow it worksTypical trade-offs
ProhibitionMost or all gambling is illegalDemand often moves to unregulated or offshore providers with no player protection
State monopolyA single government-owned or government-appointed operatorTight control and public revenue, but little competition
Competitive licensingPrivate operators apply for licenses and follow detailed rulesMore choice and oversight, but heavy compliance and enforcement work
Licensing for exportA jurisdiction licenses operators that mainly serve players elsewhereBrings business to the licensing country; recognition abroad varies

Over time, many countries have moved toward requiring a local license for any operator that serves their residents, regardless of where the company is based.

What a license typically requires

Details differ, but in well-regulated markets, operators can usually expect requirements like these.

  1. Suitability checks. Owners, executives and key staff are vetted for criminal history and financial soundness.
  2. Game approval and testing. Games and random number generators are typically tested by independent labs before release and audited afterward, to confirm outcomes are random and payouts match the stated rules. Our guide to random number generators explains what is being tested.
  3. Transparency. Rules, payouts and often return-to-player figures must be available to players.
  4. Identity and age checks. Players must be of legal age, and operators must verify who they are, partly to prevent fraud and money laundering.
  5. Protection of player funds. Some regulators require customer balances to be kept separate from company money.
  6. Responsible-gambling tools. Deposit limits, time limits, reality checks and self-exclusion are common requirements for online operators.
  7. Advertising rules. Restrictions on targeting minors and on misleading claims about winning.
  8. Complaints and enforcement. A route for disputes, plus fines or license loss for breaking the rules.

Regulators commonly named as examples include the UK Gambling Commission, the Malta Gaming Authority, the New Jersey Division of Gaming Enforcement and the Nevada Gaming Control Board. Each operates under its own laws, but the toolkit above is broadly shared.

Online gambling: regulation without borders

Land casinos are easy to supervise: they sit inside one jurisdiction. Online gambling crosses borders instantly, and that has shaped how rules evolved.

The first online casinos appeared in the mid-1990s, and Antigua and Barbuda's Free Trade and Processing Act of 1994 is often cited as an early licensing framework. Other jurisdictions followed with their own online licensing systems.

The UK passed its Gambling Act in 2005, and the UK Gambling Commission began operating in 2007, creating a single regulator for most commercial gambling in Great Britain. Many other countries have since built their own online frameworks, often requiring geolocation checks so that only players physically inside the jurisdiction can play, and sometimes blocking payments to unlicensed sites.

Two philosophies sit behind these systems. Under a point-of-supply approach, the operator needs a license where it is based. Under a point-of-consumption approach, it needs a license wherever its customers are. The broad trend has been toward the second, because it lets each country apply its own player protections and taxes to the people who live there.

The United States: a state-by-state patchwork

In the US, gambling law is largely set by individual states. Nevada legalized casino gambling in 1931. New Jersey voters approved Atlantic City casinos in 1976, and the first opened in 1978.

New Jersey also launched regulated online casino play in November 2013. In May 2018, the Supreme Court's decision in Murphy v. NCAA struck down the federal law that had largely banned sports betting, leaving each state free to decide for itself.

The result is a patchwork. What is legal in one state can be prohibited across the border, and the rules continue to change. If you want to know what applies where you live, check your state's official gaming regulator or legislature rather than relying on any general summary, including this one.

What a license can and cannot promise

A license is meaningful. In a well-regulated market, it means the game has been tested, the operator has been vetted and you have tools and a complaints process if something goes wrong.

What a license does not do is change the math. A certified slot with a 96% RTP has been verified to return about 96% over the long run. That still means a 4% house edge, every spin.

Expected cost per hour = bet × spins per hour × (1 − RTP)

At $1 per spin and 500 spins an hour, a fully certified 96% machine costs about $1 × 500 × 4% = $20 per hour on average. Fair, transparent and still a cost. You can watch that play out without any money at stake in our slot simulator.

Reality check: Unlicensed operators may offer none of these protections: no independent testing, no reliable way to resolve disputes and no responsible-gambling tools. Regulation is a floor for fairness and safety, not an endorsement of gambling, and it never turns gambling into a way to earn money.

Player protection is part of the job

Modern regulation increasingly treats harm reduction as a core duty, alongside fairness and crime prevention. Self-exclusion schemes, mandatory deposit limits, reality checks and rules about how quickly games can be played are all examples of that shift. Some markets run self-exclusion programs that cover many operators at once.

These tools work best when people use them early. Our responsible gambling guide explains how to set them up, and our help page lists support options if gambling has stopped feeling like entertainment.

The bottom line

Gambling regulation is about trust: making sure games are random, payouts match the rules, money is handled properly and players have protections. Countries reach that goal through bans, monopolies or licensing, and the online era has pushed most toward local licensing with strict checks.

Keep the limits in view, though. A regulator can guarantee that a game is fair. It cannot make the house edge disappear, and it was never meant to.

Frequently asked questions

What does a gambling regulator do?

A regulator licenses operators, checks that owners and key staff are suitable, approves and audits games, enforces rules on advertising and player protection, and handles serious complaints. Its job is to make sure gambling is fair, transparent and supervised.

Does a gambling license mean the games are fair?

In well-run regulated markets, games are typically tested so that outcomes are random and payouts match the published rules. Fair still includes a house edge, so the casino keeps its advantage over time.

Is online gambling legal in the United States?

It depends on the state. Gambling law in the US is largely set state by state, New Jersey launched regulated online casino play in 2013, and rules elsewhere vary and change, so always check the current law where you live.

Why do some gambling sites hold licenses from small countries?

Some jurisdictions license operators that serve customers in other countries. Whether that license is recognized where a player lives depends on local law, which is why many countries now require operators to hold a local license.
