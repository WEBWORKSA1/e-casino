RTP vs. Volatility: What Slot Numbers Really Mean

RTP sets the long-run cost of a slot and volatility sets the swings. Three machines with the same 96% RTP show why your session rarely looks like the average.

By e.casino Editorial TeamUpdated Nov 2, 20267 min read

Key takeaways

  • RTP is total paid out divided by total wagered over a huge number of spins; house edge = 100% minus RTP.
  • RTP applies to every dollar wagered, so a $1 game at 96% RTP and 500 spins per hour costs about $20 per hour on average.
  • Volatility is the spread of results: three 96% machines can give a 17%, 44% or 39% chance of finishing a 500-spin session ahead.
  • On high-volatility games, much of the RTP sits in rare prizes, so most players experience a lower return than the advertised figure.

RTP tells you how much a slot pays back on average over an enormous number of spins. Volatility tells you how bumpy the ride is on the way there. Two games with the same 96% RTP can give you completely different nights: one bleeds slowly and predictably, the other usually takes everything and occasionally pays big.

What RTP means, and what it doesn't

RTP, or return to player, is the total a game pays out divided by the total wagered on it, measured over the long run. It is the mirror image of the house edge.

House edge = 100% − RTP, so a 96% RTP game has a 4% edge

The key word is wagered, not deposited. A 96% RTP does not mean you walk away with $96 of your $100. It means the game keeps about 4 cents of every dollar that passes through it.

And dollars pass through quickly. Bet $1 a spin at a commonly cited pace of 500 spins an hour and you wager $500 in that hour. Expected loss: $500 × 4% = $20, even if you only sat down with $100, because winnings keep getting fed back in.

Slot RTPs commonly fall somewhere between about 85% and 98%. The figure is a theoretical average built into the game's math, not a promise about any session.

One practical wrinkle: many modern games are built in more than one RTP version, and the venue chooses which one to run. Two machines with the same theme and artwork can therefore return different amounts. Where the RTP is published, the game's help or information screen is the place to look.

What volatility measures

Volatility, sometimes called variance, describes how a game's payouts are spread around that average. Low-volatility games pay small amounts often. High-volatility games pay rarely, but the wins are large when they come.

In math terms it is the standard deviation of a single spin: how far a typical result lands from the average. RTP is the center of the target. Volatility is how widely the shots scatter.

Volatility is related to hit frequency but not the same thing. A game can hit often and still be volatile if most hits are tiny and one rare prize carries a big share of the RTP.

Three machines with the same 96% RTP

Here are three simplified machines. Each costs $1 a spin and returns exactly 96% on average. Only the way the 96 cents is delivered changes.

  • Steady: pays $2 with probability 48% (0.48 × $2 = $0.96).
  • Middle: pays $24 with probability 4% (0.04 × $24 = $0.96).
  • Spiky: pays $960 with probability 0.1% (0.001 × $960 = $0.96).

Now play each one for exactly 500 spins with a $500 budget, so you can never run dry early. Your expected result on every machine is identical: 500 × −$0.04 = −$20.

The math: For a machine with one prize of size X that hits with probability p, the standard deviation per spin is X × √(p × (1 − p)). For Spiky that is 960 × √(0.001 × 0.999) ≈ $30.34. Spiky's chance of at least one hit in 500 spins is 1 − 0.999^500 ≈ 39.4%; otherwise all $500 is gone. Steady needs at least 251 wins out of 500 at 48% to finish ahead, which happens 17.4% of the time.

Same RTP, same expected loss, wildly different experiences. On Steady, about 19 sessions in 20 finish between −$64 and +$24. On Spiky, six sessions in ten lose the entire $500 and the other four win at least $460.

The median makes the point bluntly. More than half of Spiky players lose every dollar. The 96% average is held up by the minority who hit.

Middle has the best chance of finishing ahead, yet more than one session in five loses over $100. Volatility does not just widen the range; it reshapes it.

Hit frequency moves just as dramatically. Steady pays on 48% of spins, about one in two. Middle pays on one spin in 25. Spiky pays on one spin in 1,000, so a typical 500-spin session sees no win at all. If you have ever sat through a long silent stretch on a game that promised a high RTP, this is why.

How many spins before RTP shows up?

RTP is a long-run figure, and volatility decides how long the long run is. The spread of your measured return shrinks with the square root of the number of spins N:

Spins needed to land within ±1 point of RTP about 95% of the time: N ≈ (2 × σ ÷ 0.01)²
  • Steady (σ = $1.00): about 40,000 spins, or 80 hours at 500 spins an hour.
  • Middle (σ = $4.70): about 884,000 spins, roughly 1,770 hours.
  • Spiky (σ = $30.34): about 36.8 million spins, more than 73,000 hours. That is over eight years of nonstop play.

No individual player ever reaches the long run on a high-volatility game. The RTP is real, but it describes the machine across all of its players, not your evening.

Jackpots: the part of RTP most players never collect

Large top prizes widen this gap. Suppose a game has a 94% RTP, and 3 percentage points of it come from a $30,000 prize that hits once in a million $1 spins (30,000 ÷ 1,000,000 = 0.03).

A player who makes 10,000 spins has about a 1% chance of ever seeing that prize: 1 − (1 − 1/1,000,000)^10,000 ≈ 0.995%. The other 99% are effectively playing a 91% RTP game.

Progressive jackpots work the same way, with a twist: the jackpot's share of the return depends on how large it has grown. Either way, a slice of the advertised RTP is reserved for a very small number of people.

Reality check: A higher RTP is genuinely better for you on average, but slots are designed to return less than 100%, and no RTP tells you what the next spin will do. Each spin is independent, so a machine is never due.

How to use RTP and volatility together

  • RTP sets the price. Expected loss = bet × spins × (1 − RTP). At $0.50 a spin and 400 spins an hour, a 95% game costs $0.50 × 400 × 5% = $10 an hour on average.
  • Volatility sets the swings. A volatile game needs a bigger buffer for the same playing time, and it makes a short session's result almost pure luck.
  • Neither predicts the next spin. Outcomes come from a random number generator; see how RNGs work.
  • Pick the ride you actually want. Low volatility stretches a budget and keeps results close to expectation. High volatility trades frequent losing sessions for a slim chance of a large win.
Tip: Run the free slot simulator at the same RTP with low and high volatility, and watch how differently thousands of sessions spread out.

The bottom line

RTP is the long-run average. Volatility is the distance between that average and what actually happens to you. Equal-RTP games share the same expected cost, but a high-volatility game makes your own result far less predictable, and much of its return lands with a lucky few.

Use RTP to estimate what play costs per hour, use volatility to size your buffer, and never read either one as a forecast. Set the budget before the first spin; the guide to setting a gambling budget shows how.

Frequently asked questions

Is a higher RTP always better?

On average, yes: a higher RTP means a lower expected cost per dollar wagered. It says nothing about a single session, where volatility usually matters more than a point or two of RTP.

Does high volatility mean a slot pays less?

No. Volatility describes how payouts are spread out, not how much is paid in total. A high-volatility and a low-volatility game can have exactly the same RTP and the same expected loss.

Can I measure a slot's RTP by playing it?

Not in any practical sense. Even on a very steady game you would need tens of thousands of spins to pin the RTP down to within one percentage point, and on a volatile game it can take tens of millions.

What does RTP mean for my $100?

RTP is charged on turnover, not on your deposit. At 96% RTP you lose about 4 cents per dollar wagered on average, and because you rebet winnings, $100 can generate several hundred dollars of wagers in an hour.
