Card Counting Explained: How It Works and Why It's Hard

How blackjack card counting works: the Hi-Lo system, running count vs true count, betting with the count, the insurance math and the real practical limits.

By e.casino Editorial TeamUpdated Nov 2, 20266 min read

Key takeaways

  • Blackjack has memory: cards that are dealt are gone until the shuffle, so the mix of what remains shifts the odds hand to hand.
  • The Hi-Lo count scores 2 to 6 as +1, 7 to 9 as 0 and tens and aces as -1. Divide the running count by decks remaining to get the true count.
  • A high true count means a ten-rich shoe, which favors the player. Counters bet more then and the minimum otherwise.
  • It is legal in most places, but casinos can ban counters, shuffle early or limit bets, and the edge is thin with big swings.

Card counting is a way of tracking whether the cards left in a blackjack shoe are rich in tens and aces, which help you, or in small cards, which help the dealer. Counters bet more when the remaining cards favor them. It's legal in most places and mathematically sound, but the edge is thin and casinos fight back.

Why the remaining cards matter

A roulette wheel has no memory. A blackjack shoe does: every card dealt is gone until the next shuffle, so the odds on the next hand depend on what's left.

Tens and aces left in the shoe help you for three reasons:

  • More blackjacks. You and the dealer get them equally often, but yours pay 3:2 while the dealer's only collect your bet at 1:1.
  • Better doubles. When you double on 10 or 11, a ten-value card is the card you want.
  • More dealer busts. The dealer must hit stiff hands of 12 to 16, and in a ten-rich shoe more of those hits break.

Small cards do the reverse. They rescue the dealer's stiff hands and ruin your doubles. When the shoe is full of 2s through 6s, the house edge is larger than usual.

The Hi-Lo count, step by step

Hi-Lo, one of the most widely taught systems, gives each card a tag. You keep a single running total, starting at zero after the shuffle.

Hi-Lo card values
CardsTagCards per deckWhy
2, 3, 4, 5, 6+120Low cards leaving the shoe make it richer in high cards.
7, 8, 9012Roughly neutral.
10, J, Q, K, A−120High cards leaving the shoe make it poorer.

The count is balanced: a full deck sums to +20 − 20 = 0. If you count every card through a shoe correctly, you end at exactly zero, which makes a handy self-check.

Say these eight cards come out: 5, K, 3, 9, A, 2, 6, Q. Their tags are +1, −1, +1, 0, −1, +1, +1, −1, so the running count is +1. Slightly more low cards than high cards have left, so the remaining shoe is very slightly richer in tens and aces.

Tip: Counters cancel pairs of cards instead of adding one by one. A king and a 4 on the table cancel to zero at a glance.

From running count to true count

A running count of +6 means very different things with one deck left or six. The fix is to divide by the number of decks still in the shoe.

True count = running count ÷ decks remaining

Running count +12 with 3 decks left is a true count of +4: a strongly ten-rich shoe. The same +12 with 6 decks left is only +2. Counters estimate decks remaining by glancing at the discard tray, usually to the nearest half deck.

The true count is what drives decisions. A shoe starts with a house edge of roughly 0.5% under good rules, and each point of true count shifts the edge a fraction of a percentage point toward the player. It takes a clearly positive true count before the player is actually ahead.

Turning the count into an edge

Counting does two things: it tells you when to bet more, and it tells you when to deviate from basic strategy.

Bet sizing

A counter bets the table minimum at neutral or negative counts and raises bets as the true count climbs. A hypothetical spread: $10 at +1 or below, $20 at +2, $40 at +3, $60 at +4 or higher. The profit comes from big bets on the minority of hands where the player has the edge, outweighing small losses everywhere else.

Strategy deviations: the insurance example

Insurance pays 2:1 when the dealer's hole card is a ten. If p is the share of tens among the unseen cards, the bet's expected value per $1 is:

EV = 2p − (1 − p) = 3p − 1

That's positive only when p is above 1/3. In a fresh shoe, p = 4/13 ≈ 30.8%, so insurance loses about 7.7%. But suppose 52 unseen cards contain 18 tens: p = 18/52 ≈ 34.6%, and EV = 3 × 0.346 − 1 ≈ +3.8%. At a high count, the bet basic strategy always refuses becomes a good one.

A short history

Edward O. Thorp, a mathematician, showed that blackjack could be beaten by tracking the cards and published the method in "Beat the Dealer" in 1962. Casinos responded with more decks, earlier shuffles and closer attention to players who change bet sizes sharply.

The cat-and-mouse game has continued ever since, and it explains most of the practical limits below.

Why it's hard in practice

  • Speed and accuracy. You must count every card, convert to a true count and play perfect basic strategy at full dealing speed, with noise and conversation around you. Small errors can wipe out a small edge.
  • Penetration. If the dealer shuffles with half the shoe unplayed, the high counts that pay you rarely arrive.
  • Countermeasures. Casinos can ban suspected counters from blackjack, shuffle early or cap your bets. Spreading from $10 to $60 is exactly what they watch for.
  • Machines and software. Continuous shuffling machines return cards to the mix constantly. Digital games that shuffle a fresh deck every hand leave nothing to count.
  • Bad rules. A 6:5 blackjack payout adds about 1.4% to the house edge, more than a typical counting advantage can overcome.
  • Variance. With an edge this thin, stretches of hundreds of hands in the red are normal. Without a large bankroll relative to your bets, you can go broke before the math works.
Reality check: Counting doesn't make you win every session, or even most of them. It turns a small long-run disadvantage into a small long-run advantage, under ideal conditions that casinos work to prevent. Use the bankroll calculator to see how wide the swings get even with a positive edge.

Is it legal?

Counting with your own brain is legal in most places. You're simply paying attention to cards dealt face up.

Using a device or software to count at the table is a different matter and is prohibited in many places. And legality doesn't oblige a casino to deal to you. As private businesses, casinos can ask a counter to leave the blackjack tables.

How to practice without money

  1. Master basic strategy first, until every decision is automatic. Counting without it is pointless. The free blackjack trainer drills exactly that.
  2. Count down a single deck at home, checking that you finish at zero.
  3. Practice converting running counts to true counts with different deck estimates.

Even if you never use it at a table, counting is a lovely lesson in dependent events, the opposite of the independent spins behind the gambler's fallacy.

The bottom line

Card counting works because blackjack cards are dealt without replacement, so the remaining shoe really can favor the player. The Hi-Lo count and the true count conversion are simple to learn and hard to execute perfectly.

The edge is thin, swings are large, and casinos can shuffle, limit or ban you. It's a real exception to "the house always wins," but a narrow one.

Frequently asked questions

Is card counting illegal?

Counting cards in your head is legal in most places. Casinos are private businesses, though, so they can ask a suspected counter to stop playing blackjack, shuffle early or restrict bet sizes.

Do you need to memorize every card to count?

No. A system like Hi-Lo tracks a single running number. You add or subtract one per card and never need to remember which specific cards appeared.

Does card counting work in online blackjack?

Not in standard digital games that shuffle a fresh virtual deck for every hand. With no cards carried from one hand to the next, there is nothing to count.

How much can a card counter make?

Less than most people imagine. The long-run advantage is on the same small scale as the house edge it overturns, losing stretches lasting many hours are normal, and casino countermeasures cap the upside.
