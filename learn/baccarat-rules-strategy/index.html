Baccarat Explained: Banker vs. Player and the Tie Trap

How baccarat works: card values, the third-card rules, why Banker wins more often, the 5% commission and why the Tie bet carries a 14.36% house edge.

By e.casino Editorial TeamUpdated Nov 2, 20266 min read

Key takeaways

  • Baccarat has no decisions after you bet. You choose Banker, Player or Tie, and fixed rules play both hands.
  • Banker wins about 45.86% of hands, Player about 44.62%, and about 9.52% tie. Ties push Banker and Player bets.
  • With a 5% commission on winning Banker bets, the house edge is 1.06% on Banker and 1.24% on Player.
  • The Tie bet at 8:1 carries a 14.36% house edge, more than ten times the cost of Banker.

Baccarat is the simplest game in the casino to play: you pick Banker, Player or Tie, and fixed rules do everything else. Banker is the best bet at a 1.06% house edge even after its 5% commission. Player costs 1.24%. The Tie, with its tempting 8:1 payout, costs 14.36%.

Card values and totals

Baccarat totals run from 0 to 9, and only the last digit counts.

  • Ace = 1.
  • 2 through 9 = face value.
  • 10, jack, queen, king = 0.

A 7 and an 8 make 15, which counts as 5. A king and a 9 make 9, the best possible total. You can't bust; the total just wraps around past 9.

"Player" and "Banker" are only names for the two hands. You don't play as either, and anyone at the table can bet on either one.

How a hand is dealt

  1. Bets. You put chips on Banker, Player or Tie.
  2. Two cards each. The Player hand and the Banker hand each get two cards.
  3. Naturals. If either hand totals 8 or 9, it's a natural. Both hands stand and the higher total wins.
  4. Player's third card. Otherwise, Player draws a third card on 0 to 5 and stands on 6 or 7.
  5. Banker's third card. Banker then follows its own rule, shown below.
  6. Settle. The total closer to 9 wins. Equal totals are a tie.

Most casino baccarat is the version often called punto banco, where every draw is automatic and the dealer handles it all. You never have to memorize the rules to play, but knowing them explains the odds.

Because nobody makes choices, a hand plays out in seconds. That speed matters later, when small edges get multiplied by dozens of hands an hour.

The third-card rules

If Player stood with two cards, Banker uses the same rule as Player: draw on 0 to 5, stand on 6 or 7. If Player drew, Banker's move depends on its own total and on the value of Player's third card.

A worked hand: Player gets 4 and king (4). Banker gets 3 and 2 (5). Player draws a 7, making 11, which counts as 1. Banker has 5 and Player's third card was a 7, so Banker draws: a 9, making 14, which counts as 4. Banker's 4 beats Player's 1.

Banker, Player or Tie: the numbers

With eight decks and a 5% commission on winning Banker bets, the long-run results look like this:

When the hands tie, Banker and Player bets push: your stake comes back untouched.

The math: Per $1 on Banker, you win $0.95 with probability 0.4586 and lose $1 with probability 0.4462 (ties push). EV ≈ 0.95 × 0.4586 − 0.4462 ≈ −0.0105, about 1.06% once rounding is removed. Per $1 on Player: 0.4462 − 0.4586 ≈ −0.0124, or 1.24%.

Why Banker wins more often

Banker acts last. Its drawing rules take Player's third card into account, so Banker's decisions are made with more information than Player's one-size-fits-all rule.

That gives Banker a slight edge in head-to-head results: 45.86% versus 44.62%. If Banker bets paid a straight 1:1, bettors would have the advantage. The 5% commission exists to take that back, plus a little more.

Here's what that looks like over an average 100 hands at $10 each, with no commission at all: about 45.86 Banker wins, 44.62 losses and 9.52 pushes. That nets 45.86 × $10 − 44.62 × $10 = +$12.40 for the Banker bettor.

Now take 5% off each win. The wins are worth 45.86 × $9.50 ≈ $435.67, the losses cost $446.20, and the result flips to about −$10.53 on $1,000 wagered. That's the roughly 1.06% house edge, built from one small fee.

In practice, a $100 Banker win pays $95. Some tables track the commission you owe with markers and collect it at the end of the shoe.

The tie trap

An 8:1 payout looks generous. But ties happen only about 9.52% of the time, roughly one hand in 10.5, and a fair payout for that would be close to 9.5:1.

Tie EV per $1 = 8 × P(tie) − 1 × P(no tie) = 8 × 0.0952 − 0.9048 ≈ −0.143

Use unrounded probabilities and you get the 14.36% house edge. That's more than 13 times the cost of a Banker bet, for a bet that loses about nine times in ten.

Reality check: The tie bet's big payout is exactly what makes it expensive. If you see a table paying 9:1 on ties, the edge drops to 4.84%, which is better, but still about four and a half times the Banker bet.

Patterns, scorecards and "systems"

Baccarat tables often offer scorecards, and some display boards that chart the history of the shoe in rows and columns. Players use them to spot "streaks" and "chops" and bet accordingly.

The history is real; its predictive power isn't. A run of six Banker wins tells you nothing useful about the seventh hand. Believing otherwise is the gambler's fallacy, and no pattern changes the 1.06% edge on Banker or the 1.24% on Player.

What about counting cards, as in blackjack? Baccarat is dealt from a shoe without replacement, so the mix of remaining cards does shift slightly as play goes on. But there are no playing decisions to adjust, and on the Banker and Player bets those shifts are generally too small to exploit. Bet size is the only lever, and it has almost nothing to grip.

Betting progressions fail for the same reason they fail at roulette: every bet in the sequence has a negative expected value, so the sequence does too.

What baccarat costs per hour

A baccarat table deals about 70 hands an hour. At $25 a hand, that's $1,750 wagered:

  • Banker every hand: $1,750 × 1.06% ≈ $18.55 expected loss per hour.
  • Player every hand: $1,750 × 1.24% ≈ $21.70.
  • Tie every hand: $1,750 × 14.36% ≈ $251.30.

Tip: Watch for side bets and rule variants with different payouts, such as tables that alter how a winning Banker hand is paid. Any change to a payout changes the house edge, so check before assuming the standard figures apply.

The bottom line

Baccarat is pure chance once your chips are down, which makes bet selection your only lever. Banker at 1.06% is the cheapest option, Player at 1.24% is close behind, and the Tie at 8:1 is a 14.36% trap.

No scorecard, streak or system moves those numbers. If you play, Banker and Player are the only bets worth considering. Skip the tie, and set a budget you're comfortable spending on entertainment.

Frequently asked questions

Is it better to bet Banker or Player in baccarat?

Banker, slightly. Even after the 5% commission, Banker has a 1.06% house edge versus 1.24% for Player, because the Banker hand wins more often.

Why do casinos charge commission on Banker bets?

Because the Banker hand acts last and wins more often than Player. Without the commission, an even-money Banker bet would favor the bettor, so the 5% fee restores the house edge.

Should I ever bet on the Tie?

From a math standpoint, no. At the usual 8:1 payout the Tie bet carries a 14.36% house edge. Even at 9:1 it is 4.84%, still far worse than Banker.

Do baccarat patterns or scorecards help predict results?

No. Past results do not tell you which hand will win next. Scorecards record history; they do not forecast it.
